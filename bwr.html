Automatic control

The feed pump speed, the feed valve, the hotwell makeup and the steam valve each have an M/A switch. In A the slider follows the automatic demand and is greyed out. In M the slider is yours and a notch marks the demand: your value plus the correction the controller would make for the present pressure or level error. It sits on your value when the error is zero, and switching to A starts from the notch. Net heat, the extraction valve and the safety/relief valves are always manual; the valves' springs open them on pressure whatever you do. Nothing switches between M and A by itself.

Pressure regulator (steam valve)

Proportional-integral on dome pressure: 0.1 MPa above the setpoint opens the valve a further 30 %, and the integral (30 s) removes the offset. Below the setpoint it closes the valve, so with little heat the pressure rises to the setpoint and then holds there.

Feedwater level control (feed valve)

Three-element: the feed flow demand follows the main steam flow, plus a proportional-integral term on the level error (800 kg/s per metre, integral 60 s). A flow controller then moves the feed valve until the measured feed flow matches the demand (proportional-integral, integral 4 s). Steam through the safety/relief valves is not in the steam-flow signal, so the level term has to make up for it. The valve can only pass what the pump delivers: if the vessel pressure rises past the pump's shutoff head, no valve opening helps.

Feed pump speed

Proportional-integral on the pump discharge pressure minus dome pressure, as turbine-driven feed pumps are run: the speed follows the vessel pressure, so the feed valve always has about the same pressure drop to work with (2.2 MPa at rated, 0.3 MPa of it in the heater and piping). 0.1 MPa of error moves the speed 0.15 %, integral 10 s. The speed changes at most 10 % per second.

Hotwell level (makeup)

Makeup from the condensate storage tank, or reject to it when negative, holds the hotwell half full: feed flow minus condensate return, plus a proportional-integral term on the hotwell level (10 % low asks for the full 100 kg/s, integral 300 s).

Things to try

  1. Startup. The vessel is at 100 °C and atmospheric pressure with the head vent just shut, the level low at 11.5 m, every valve shut, the feed pump stopped and 30 MW of heat. Every control starts in A except the feed valve, which starts in M and shut: the level controller can only add water, and the water expands about 3 m on the way to 7 MPa, so in A it would fill the vessel to the steam nozzle. Open it by hand once the steam valve is passing steam, or the level boils down to the fuel in about 4 hours; switch it to A once the level has stopped swelling. The water heats at about 80 °C per hour, faster than the usual 55 °C/h limit, so turn the heat down to stay inside it. Pressure follows the saturation curve, and the level rises as the water expands: about 3 m on the way to 7 MPa. The steam valve in A opens when pressure reaches 7 MPa and holds it there. The pump speed in A runs the pump just fast enough to keep its discharge 2.2 MPa above the vessel, so the feed valve has a sensible pressure drop to work with. At full speed it would push over 4000 kg/s into a vessel at atmospheric pressure. The pump develops at most 10 MPa at full speed (6.4 MPa at 80 %), so near 7 MPa it needs more than 84 % speed to deliver anything.
  2. Rated power. Every control starts in A, balanced: pump at full speed, feed valve 75 % open, heater at 216 °C. Change the net heat and watch the controllers follow. Less heat means less steam made in the core, fewer voids and a lower level (shrink); the feedwater controller sees the level fall and the steam flow drop at the same time. Less steam also means less extraction steam, so the feed arrives colder: at 2000 MW about 188 °C.
  3. Close main steam valve at rated power. The valve strokes shut in 5 s, pressure climbs, the voids collapse and the level drops about 0.7 m within seconds. The heat stays on, because there is no scram or neutronics: all eleven safety/relief valves open on their springs at 7.75 MPa pass about 1130 kg/s, less than the 1790 kg/s the core makes, so pressure keeps climbing. Within about 30 s it passes the feed pump's 10 MPa shutoff head: the feed valve opens wide in A, but no water gets in, and the core uncovers after about 80 s. Press Scram as the valve closes: only the lowest bank of four lifts, pressure peaks just above 7.6 MPa, and they cycle as in a real isolation. With no steam to the turbine there is no extraction steam, so the feed cools to the hotwell's 46 °C and the level controller overshoots by about 1.2 m before it settles.
  4. Isolation at 200 MW (about 6 % of rated, decay heat shortly after a scram). The steam valve starts in M and closes; everything else is in A. and the four valves set lowest (7.62 MPa) lift on their springs and cycle, about once every 40 s. Each lift drops the pressure 0.25 MPa and blows inventory to the suppression pool. The feed valve in A keeps the level, and makeup in A tops up the hotwell for the steam that went to the pool instead of back to it; switch makeup to M at zero and watch the hotwell drain. Open one or two valves by hand to depressurise steadily, as for a cooldown: one valve at 20 MW takes the vessel from 7 to about 1.6 MPa in half an hour.
  5. Lose feedwater heating at rated power with everything in A. The extraction valve throttles to 20 %, the heater shell pressure drops, and the feed reaches the vessel about 35 K colder within a minute, 54 K colder after a few. Colder feedwater takes more heat to bring to saturation, so about 12 % less steam is made and the voids shrink while the controllers follow. With everything in M the steam valve passes the old steam flow, so pressure falls, the pump delivers more against the lower pressure, and the vessel floods in under 3 minutes. In a real BWR the colder water also raises reactor power through void feedback; here the heat is what you set.
  6. Depressurise. At hot standby open the steam valve wide by hand. The water flashes as the pressure falls, the voids grow and the level rises at first, before the lost inventory brings it down.

What the model solves

The vessel (600 m³ of free volume, 21 m tall) holds water and steam at one pressure. It tracks the total mass and energy, and the steam mass. Each step it finds the pressure at which the water and the saturated steam exactly fill the vessel. Water hotter than saturation at that pressure flashes at once, so a falling pressure makes steam throughout the water. Water below saturation, after cold feedwater or cooling, condenses steam at a finite rate (10 s time constant), so pressure drops after a cold-water injection.

The steam the water makes passes through it as bubbles before it reaches the dome. These voids take up volume inside the two-phase mixture, so they raise the level without changing the pressure. They clear with a 0.7 s time constant, sized to give about 35 m³ of voids, about 1.2 m of swell, at rated power. The level shown is the height of water plus voids over the vessel's mean cross-section; the recorder also shows the collapsed level, water alone.

Net heat goes into the water: core power or decay heat minus cleanup and shutdown cooling, with the vessel's own heat losses included. Steam leaves through the main steam line and the safety/relief valves at saturated-steam enthalpy; feedwater enters as compressed liquid at the heater outlet temperature.

The feedwater train is a hotwell, one pump, one heater and the feed valve. The flow is where the pump's pressure (10 MPa at full speed, falling with flow and with the square of speed) equals the vessel pressure plus the drops in the heater, the piping and the valve, so the same valve opening passes more water when the vessel pressure falls. The heater stands for the whole heater train: a shell in which steam extracted from the turbine condenses on the feed tubes. The extraction pressure rises in proportion to the main steam flow (2.4 MPa at rated). The extraction valve's opening sets how much steam reaches the shell, and so the shell pressure; the feed leaves 3 K below the shell's saturation temperature, 216 °C at rated with the valve open. With no steam to the turbine there is no heating, and the feed comes in at the hotwell temperature.

Assumptions and limits

  • No neutronics: power is the net heat you set and does not respond to voids, pressure or feedwater temperature. Scram is a button only: nothing trips it automatically (real plants scram on valve closure, high pressure, low level). After a scram the heat follows a fixed curve: the fission power falls with the rods in (a prompt drop, then 10 s and 80 s delayed-neutron tails) and decay heat of 6.5 % falls as (1 + t)^−0.2, about 7 % of the pre-scram heat at 10 s, 3.3 % at 1 min, 1.3 % at 1 h, assuming long operation at that heat. No recirculation pumps or flow control, no jet pumps. The voids clear at the same rate at every power and pressure; in a real core they depend on recirculation flow, and the bubbles from bulk flashing in a depressurisation rise more slowly, so swell on depressurisation is underestimated.
  • The water is one well-mixed region: downcomer, core and lower plenum share one temperature. In a real BWR the downcomer is a few kelvin subcooled at power and the core still boils; here the water is saturated whenever it boils.
  • The steam is always saturated, so there is no superheat in the dome. The dome holds no air or nitrogen: below 100 °C the pressure would fall below atmospheric, where a real vessel would have its head vent open.
  • Level is the volume of water and voids over a uniform cross-section of 28.6 m², from the inside bottom of the vessel: top of active fuel 9.3 m, normal level 14.3 m, main steam nozzle 16.5 m. Real vessels measure level in the downcomer, against a reference leg. The model stops if the level falls to the top of the fuel or reaches the steam nozzle.
  • The main steam valve stands for the main steam isolation valves together with the turbine control and bypass valves: 2000 kg/s fully open at 7.0 MPa, in proportion to pressure (choked), into a condenser at 0.01 MPa. It strokes at most 20 % per second. Turbine, condenser and moisture separator reheaters are not modelled.
  • Safety/relief valves: eleven, each with a spring set pressure and a rated capacity from a GE BWR/4 training lesson plan: four at 1090 psig (7.62 MPa abs) rated 800,000 lb/h (101 kg/s), four at 1100 psig (7.69 MPa) 808,000 lb/h, three at 1110 psig (7.75 MPa) 815,000 lb/h. Hover a valve's button for its opening pressure and capacity. A spring opens its valve fully above the set pressure and recloses it 0.25 MPa lower; that blowdown is an illustrative figure, not from the source. Flow scales in proportion to pressure from the rated capacity, into a suppression pool at 0.1 MPa that does not heat up. Opening by hand stands for the valve's relief (pneumatic) mode; there is no automatic relief logic, no low-low set and no automatic depressurisation system.
  • Feedwater: there is no condenser yet. The main steam comes back to the hotwell at once as water at 45.8 °C (saturation at the condenser's 0.01 MPa), so the hotwell only changes with makeup, feed, and steam lost through the safety/relief valves. The hotwell holds 600 t; makeup from the condensate storage tank, or reject to it, is up to 100 kg/s. The condensate, booster and feed pumps are one pump with an illustrative curve (10 MPa shutoff at full speed), no trips, no minimum-flow line and no startup valve; below 5 % hotwell it loses suction gradually. One heater stage stands for the train, with one throttling valve on its extraction line; real plants have several low- and high-pressure stages whose extraction lines carry only isolation and non-return valves. The outlet follows with a 60 s lag. The drains go to the hotwell with the condensate. The cleanup (reject) flow is not modelled, so there is no way to drain water during heat-up other than the valves.
  • No vessel or internals heat capacity: the steel would slow heat-up and pressure changes. Generic numbers for a large BWR (about 3300 MW thermal); not any specific plant. All pressures are absolute.
