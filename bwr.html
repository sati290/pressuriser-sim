Automatic control

The feed pump speed, the feed valve, the hotwell makeup, the turbine control valve and the bypass valve each have an M/A switch. In A the slider follows the automatic demand and is greyed out. In M the slider is yours and a notch marks the demand: your value plus the correction the controller would make for the present pressure or level error. It sits on your value when the error is zero, and switching to A starts from the notch. Net heat, the extraction valve, the isolation and stop valves and the safety/relief valves are always manual; the valves' springs open them on pressure whatever you do. Nothing switches between M and A by itself.

Pressure regulator (control and bypass valves)

As on the GE electro-hydraulic control, simplified: a proportional-integral term on dome pressure gives a steam flow demand (0.1 MPa above the setpoint asks for 34 % of rated steam more, integral 30 s). The turbine control valve gets the demand up to the load limit; the bypass valve gets what the turbine is not taking: the excess over the load limit, or everything once the stop valve shuts. Each demand becomes a valve position through the valve's characteristic at the rated throttle pressure. Below the setpoint both valves close, so with little heat the pressure rises to the setpoint and then holds there. A real regulator senses the throttle pressure at the turbine; this one senses the dome.

Feedwater level control (feed valve)

Three-element: the feed flow demand follows the main steam flow, plus a proportional-integral term on the level error (800 kg/s per metre, integral 60 s). A flow controller then moves the feed valve until the measured feed flow matches the demand (proportional-integral, integral 4 s). Steam through the safety/relief valves is not in the steam-flow signal, so the level term has to make up for it. The valve can only pass what the pump delivers: if the vessel pressure rises past the pump's shutoff head, no valve opening helps.

Feed pump speed

Proportional-integral on the pump discharge pressure minus dome pressure, as turbine-driven feed pumps are run: the speed follows the vessel pressure, so the feed valve always has about the same pressure drop to work with (2.2 MPa at rated, 0.3 MPa of it in the heater and piping). 0.1 MPa of error moves the speed 0.15 %, integral 10 s. The speed changes at most 10 % per second.

Hotwell level (makeup)

Makeup from the condensate storage tank, or reject to it when negative, holds the hotwell half full: feed flow minus condensate return, plus a proportional-integral term on the hotwell level (10 % low asks for the full 100 kg/s, integral 300 s).

Things to try

  1. Startup. The vessel is at 100 °C and atmospheric pressure with the head vent just shut, the level low at 11.5 m, the main steam isolation valves open but the turbine stop valve, control and bypass valves shut, the feed pump stopped and 30 MW of heat. Every control starts in A except the feed valve, which starts in M and shut: the level controller can only add water, and the water expands about 3 m on the way to 7 MPa, so in A it would fill the vessel to the steam nozzle. Open it by hand once the bypass valve is passing steam, or the level boils down to the fuel in about 4 hours; switch it to A once the level has stopped swelling. The water heats at about 80 °C per hour, faster than the usual 55 °C/h limit, so turn the heat down to stay inside it. Pressure follows the saturation curve, and the level rises as the water expands: about 3 m on the way to 7 MPa. The bypass valve in A opens when pressure reaches 7 MPa and holds it there, sending the steam to the condenser; open the stop valve and the control valve in A takes the steam to the turbine as the bypass closes. The condenser has no air model yet: its vacuum is there from the start. The pump speed in A runs the pump just fast enough to keep its discharge 2.2 MPa above the vessel, so the feed valve has a sensible pressure drop to work with. At full speed it would push over 4000 kg/s into a vessel at atmospheric pressure. The pump develops at most 10 MPa at full speed (6.4 MPa at 80 %), so near 7 MPa it needs more than 84 % speed to deliver anything.
  2. Rated power. Every control starts in A, balanced: pump at full speed, feed valve 75 % open, heater at 216 °C. Change the net heat and watch the controllers follow. Less heat means less steam made in the core, fewer voids and a lower level (shrink); the feedwater controller sees the level fall and the steam flow drop at the same time. Less steam also means less extraction steam, so the feed arrives colder: at 2000 MW about 188 °C.
  3. Turbine trip at rated power, everything in A. The stop valve shuts in 0.2 s and the bypass valve opens fully within about a second, but it passes only 25 % of rated steam: pressure climbs, and the safety/relief valves lift for the rest. Without a scram all eleven open within about 20 s and still cannot hold the pressure. Press Scram as well, and the bypass alone soon carries the decay heat back to the condenser, holding 7 MPa; the safety/relief valves stay shut, partly because the model has no void-collapse power spike (no neutronics). Set the load limit below 100 % at rated power instead, and the bypass takes the steam the turbine may not.
  4. Close MSIVs at rated power. Both isolation valves stroke shut in 4 s, pressure climbs, the voids collapse and the level drops about 0.7 m within seconds. The heat stays on, because there is no scram or neutronics: all eleven safety/relief valves open on their springs at 7.75 MPa pass about 1130 kg/s, less than the 1790 kg/s the core makes, so pressure keeps climbing. Within about 30 s it passes the feed pump's 10 MPa shutoff head: the feed valve opens wide in A, but no water gets in, and the core uncovers after about 80 s. Press Scram as the valve closes: only the lowest bank of four lifts, pressure peaks just above 7.6 MPa, and they cycle as in a real isolation. With no steam to the turbine there is no extraction steam, so the feed cools to the hotwell's temperature, which falls towards the circulating water's as the condenser unloads and the level controller overshoots by about 1.2 m before it settles.
  5. Isolation at 200 MW (about 6 % of rated, decay heat shortly after a scram). The MSIVs close (4 s) and nothing more reaches the condenser; everything else is in A, so the control and bypass valves open wide on the dead header. The four valves set lowest (7.62 MPa) lift on their springs and cycle, about once every 40 s. Each lift drops the pressure 0.25 MPa and blows inventory to the suppression pool. The feed valve in A keeps the level, and makeup in A tops up the hotwell for the steam that went to the pool instead of back to it; switch makeup to M at zero and watch the hotwell drain. Open one or two valves by hand to depressurise steadily, as for a cooldown: one valve at 20 MW takes the vessel from 7 to about 1.6 MPa in half an hour.
  6. Lose feedwater heating at rated power with everything in A. The extraction valve throttles to 20 %, the heater shell pressure drops, and the feed reaches the vessel about 35 K colder within a minute, 54 K colder after a few. Colder feedwater takes more heat to bring to saturation, so about 12 % less steam is made and the voids shrink while the controllers follow. With everything in M the control valve passes the old steam flow, so pressure falls, the pump delivers more against the lower pressure, and the vessel floods in under 3 minutes. In a real BWR the colder water also raises reactor power through void feedback; here the heat is what you set.
  7. Depressurise. At hot standby (stop valve shut, the bypass passing the steam) switch the bypass valve to M and open it wide: it passes about 450 kg/s. The water flashes as the pressure falls, the voids grow and the level rises at first, before the lost inventory brings it down.

What the model solves

The vessel (600 m³ of free volume, 21 m tall) holds water and steam at one pressure. It tracks the total mass and energy, and the steam mass. Each step it finds the pressure at which the water and the saturated steam exactly fill the vessel. Water hotter than saturation at that pressure flashes at once, so a falling pressure makes steam throughout the water. Water below saturation, after cold feedwater or cooling, condenses steam at a finite rate (10 s time constant), so pressure drops after a cold-water injection.

The steam the water makes passes through it as bubbles before it reaches the dome. These voids take up volume inside the two-phase mixture, so they raise the level without changing the pressure. They clear with a 0.7 s time constant, sized to give about 35 m³ of voids, about 1.2 m of swell, at rated power. The level shown is the height of water plus voids over the vessel's mean cross-section; the recorder also shows the collapsed level, water alone.

Net heat goes into the water: core power or decay heat minus cleanup and shutdown cooling, with the vessel's own heat losses included. Steam leaves through the main steam line and the safety/relief valves at saturated-steam enthalpy; feedwater enters as compressed liquid at the heater outlet temperature.

The feedwater train is a hotwell, one pump, one heater and the feed valve. The flow is where the pump's pressure (10 MPa at full speed, falling with flow and with the square of speed) equals the vessel pressure plus the drops in the heater, the piping and the valve, so the same valve opening passes more water when the vessel pressure falls. The heater stands for the whole heater train: a shell in which steam extracted from the turbine condenses on the feed tubes. The extraction pressure rises in proportion to the main steam flow (2.4 MPa at rated). The extraction valve's opening sets how much steam reaches the shell, and so the shell pressure; the feed leaves 3 K below the shell's saturation temperature, 216 °C at rated with the valve open. With no steam to the turbine there is no heating, and the feed comes in at the hotwell temperature.

Assumptions and limits

  • No neutronics: power is the net heat you set and does not respond to voids, pressure or feedwater temperature. Scram is a button only: nothing trips it automatically (real plants scram on valve closure, high pressure, low level). After a scram the heat follows a fixed curve: the fission power falls with the rods in (a prompt drop, then 10 s and 80 s delayed-neutron tails) and decay heat of 6.5 % falls as (1 + t)^−0.2, about 7 % of the pre-scram heat at 10 s, 3.3 % at 1 min, 1.3 % at 1 h, assuming long operation at that heat. No recirculation pumps or flow control, no jet pumps. The voids clear at the same rate at every power and pressure; in a real core they depend on recirculation flow, and the bubbles from bulk flashing in a depressurisation rise more slowly, so swell on depressurisation is underestimated.
  • The water is one well-mixed region: downcomer, core and lower plenum share one temperature. In a real BWR the downcomer is a few kelvin subcooled at power and the core still boils; here the water is saturated whenever it boils.
  • The steam is always saturated, so there is no superheat in the dome. The dome holds no air or nitrogen: below 100 °C the pressure would fall below atmospheric, where a real vessel would have its head vent open.
  • Level is the volume of water and voids over a uniform cross-section of 28.6 m², from the inside bottom of the vessel: top of active fuel 9.3 m, normal level 14.3 m, main steam nozzle 16.5 m. Real vessels measure level in the downcomer, against a reference leg. The model stops if the level falls to the top of the fuel or reaches the steam nozzle.
  • Main steam line: one line with an inboard and an outboard isolation valve (MSIV), then a header that feeds the turbine through the stop valve and the control valve, and the condenser through the bypass valve. The pipes hold no steam: the header pressure is where what the line and the MSIVs pass (0.3 MPa below the dome at rated flow) equals what the valves beyond pass (choked, in proportion to header pressure). Turbine valves wide open pass 105 % of rated steam; the control valve is about 86 % open at rated, with an illustrative characteristic that flattens near full open. The bypass passes 25 % of rated, straight to the condenser, where it arrives superheated. MSIVs stroke in 4 s, the stop valve trips shut in 0.2 s and opens in 5 s, the control valve strokes in 5 s and the bypass in 0.5 s; MSIV flow area is in proportion to its position, so most of the flow is cut in the last part of the stroke. Line drop, valve sizes and stroke times other than the MSIV closure are illustrative, not from one plant. Nothing trips the turbine or closes the MSIVs automatically yet: no reactor protection.
  • Turbine stub: there is no turbine model yet. The steam expands along one fixed line from the throttle to the condenser pressure with an isentropic efficiency of 0.85, the heater's extraction steam leaving on the way, and the gross output is that work times 0.985. No moisture separator reheater, no shaft speed or stage pressures. With one heater stage drawing 46 % of the steam at 2.4 MPa the output is about 920 MWe at rated, lower than the 1100 MWe of a real plant with several heater stages and reheat.
  • Condenser: one steam space of 4000 m³ over the hotwell, saturated at its own temperature. The exhaust and the heater drains flash or condense into it, and the tube bundle condenses steam at a rate set by the circulating water: 38 m³/s at 20 °C, fixed for now, through a bundle sized (not sourced) for about 7 kPa at rated. The condensate falls into the hotwell at the shell temperature, so the hotwell and the feed pump suction follow the load: 39 °C at rated, colder at low load. No air in the shell yet. Above 0.135 MPa the LP turbine exhaust diaphragms would blow, and the model stops.
  • Safety/relief valves: eleven, each with a spring set pressure and a rated capacity from a GE BWR/4 training lesson plan: four at 1090 psig (7.62 MPa abs) rated 800,000 lb/h (101 kg/s), four at 1100 psig (7.69 MPa) 808,000 lb/h, three at 1110 psig (7.75 MPa) 815,000 lb/h. Hover a valve's button for its opening pressure and capacity. A spring opens its valve fully above the set pressure and recloses it 0.25 MPa lower; that blowdown is an illustrative figure, not from the source. Flow scales in proportion to pressure from the rated capacity, into a suppression pool at 0.1 MPa that does not heat up. Opening by hand stands for the valve's relief (pneumatic) mode; there is no automatic relief logic, no low-low set and no automatic depressurisation system.
  • Feedwater: the hotwell is the water under the condenser; it changes with the condensate, makeup, feed, and steam lost through the safety/relief valves. It holds 600 t; makeup from the condensate storage tank, or reject to it, is up to 100 kg/s. The condensate, booster and feed pumps are one pump with an illustrative curve (10 MPa shutoff at full speed), no trips, no minimum-flow line and no startup valve; below 5 % hotwell it loses suction gradually. One heater stage stands for the train, with one throttling valve on its extraction line; real plants have several low- and high-pressure stages whose extraction lines carry only isolation and non-return valves. The outlet follows with a 60 s lag. The heater drains go to the condenser, which flashes them. The cleanup (reject) flow is not modelled, so there is no way to drain water during heat-up other than the valves.
  • Plant layout: the mimic draws the whole plant as a draft. Solid parts are modelled; dashed grey parts (containment, recirculation pump and jet pump, moisture separator reheater, circulating water, air ejector and its motive steam, vacuum pump, condensate pump, polisher, LP heater) are drawn only and do nothing yet. The heater's extraction is drawn from the HP turbine. The HP and LP turbines and the generator are drawn solid for the turbine stub.
  • No vessel or internals heat capacity: the steel would slow heat-up and pressure changes. Generic numbers for a large BWR (about 3300 MW thermal); not any specific plant. All pressures are absolute.
