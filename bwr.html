Automatic control

Feedwater flow, the steam valve and the safety/relief valves each have an M/A switch. In A the slider follows the automatic demand and is greyed out. In M the slider is yours and a notch marks the demand: your value plus the correction the controller would make for the present pressure or level error. It sits on your value when the error is zero, and switching to A starts from the notch. Net heat and feedwater temperature are always manual. Nothing switches between M and A by itself.

Pressure regulator (steam valve)

Proportional-integral on dome pressure: 0.1 MPa above the setpoint opens the valve a further 30 %, and the integral (30 s) removes the offset. Below the setpoint it closes the valve, so with little heat the pressure rises to the setpoint and then holds there.

Feedwater level control

Three-element: feedwater follows the main steam flow, plus a proportional-integral term on the level error (800 kg/s per metre, integral 60 s). Steam through the safety/relief valves is not in the steam-flow signal, so the level term has to make up for it: after the steam valve closes, about 3 m below the setpoint calls for the full 2500 kg/s.

Safety/relief valves

In A, eleven valves in three groups (4, 4 and 3) open at this pressure, 0.07 MPa and 0.14 MPa above it, and each group recloses 0.35 MPa below its opening pressure. Safety mode, the spring, opens every valve above 8.5 MPa and recloses below 8.1 MPa, always armed.

Things to try

  1. Startup. The vessel is at 100 °C and atmospheric pressure with the head vent just shut, the level low at 11.5 m, every valve shut and 30 MW of heat. The water heats at about 80 °C per hour, faster than the usual 55 °C/h limit, so turn the heat down to stay inside it. Pressure follows the saturation curve, and the level rises as the water expands: about 3 m on the way to 7 MPa. When pressure nears 7 MPa, switch the steam valve to A to hold it.
  2. Rated power. Every control starts in M, balanced. Switch feedwater and the steam valve to A, then change the net heat. Less heat means less steam made in the core, fewer voids and a lower level (shrink); the feedwater controller sees the level fall and the steam flow drop at the same time.
  3. Close main steam valve at rated power. The valve strokes shut in 5 s, pressure climbs, the voids collapse and the level drops about 0.7 m within seconds. The heat stays on, because there is no scram or neutronics: at 8.5 MPa the eleven valves pass about 1300 kg/s, less than the 1790 kg/s the core makes, so pressure climbs until their flow catches up, near 11.8 MPa, far above any real plant's limit. All that steam is lost to the pool: with feedwater in A the level drops about 1.3 m and the controller runs feed up to replace it, since the steam-flow signal sees none of it; with feedwater in M the core uncovers in about 90 s. Press Scram to drop the heat to decay heat: pressure then peaks around the relief setpoints and the relief valves cycle, as in a real isolation.
  4. Isolation at 200 MW (about 6 % of rated, decay heat shortly after a scram). The valve closes, the relief valves lift in groups and cycle. Each lift drops the pressure 0.35 MPa and blows inventory to the suppression pool. Feedwater in A keeps the level. Put the relief valves in M and hold one or two open to depressurise steadily, as for a cooldown.
  5. Lose feedwater heating at rated power with feedwater and the steam valve in A. Colder feedwater takes more heat to bring to saturation, so about 12 % less steam is made, the voids shrink and the level dips briefly while the controllers follow. With everything in M the valve passes the old steam flow, so pressure falls, and the feedwater, no longer matched, fills the vessel. In a real BWR the colder water also raises reactor power through void feedback; here the heat is what you set.
  6. Depressurise. At hot standby open the steam valve wide by hand. The water flashes as the pressure falls, the voids grow and the level rises at first, before the lost inventory brings it down.

What the model solves

The vessel (600 m³ of free volume, 21 m tall) holds water and steam at one pressure. It tracks the total mass and energy, and the steam mass. Each step it finds the pressure at which the water and the saturated steam exactly fill the vessel. Water hotter than saturation at that pressure flashes at once, so a falling pressure makes steam throughout the water. Water below saturation, after cold feedwater or cooling, condenses steam at a finite rate (10 s time constant), so pressure drops after a cold-water injection.

The steam the water makes passes through it as bubbles before it reaches the dome. These voids take up volume inside the two-phase mixture, so they raise the level without changing the pressure. They clear with a 0.7 s time constant, sized to give about 35 m³ of voids, about 1.2 m of swell, at rated power. The level shown is the height of water plus voids over the vessel's mean cross-section; the recorder also shows the collapsed level, water alone.

Net heat goes into the water: core power or decay heat minus cleanup and shutdown cooling, with the vessel's own heat losses included. Steam leaves through the main steam line and the safety/relief valves at saturated-steam enthalpy; feedwater enters as compressed liquid at the temperature you set.

Assumptions and limits

  • No neutronics: power is the net heat you set and does not respond to voids, pressure or feedwater temperature. Scram is a button only: nothing trips it automatically (real plants scram on valve closure, high pressure, low level). After a scram the heat follows a fixed curve: the fission power falls with the rods in (a prompt drop, then 10 s and 80 s delayed-neutron tails) and decay heat of 6.5 % falls as (1 + t)^−0.2, about 7 % of the pre-scram heat at 10 s, 3.3 % at 1 min, 1.3 % at 1 h, assuming long operation at that heat. No recirculation pumps or flow control, no jet pumps. The voids clear at the same rate at every power and pressure; in a real core they depend on recirculation flow, and the bubbles from bulk flashing in a depressurisation rise more slowly, so swell on depressurisation is underestimated.
  • The water is one well-mixed region: downcomer, core and lower plenum share one temperature. In a real BWR the downcomer is a few kelvin subcooled at power and the core still boils; here the water is saturated whenever it boils.
  • The steam is always saturated, so there is no superheat in the dome. The dome holds no air or nitrogen: below 100 °C the pressure would fall below atmospheric, where a real vessel would have its head vent open.
  • Level is the volume of water and voids over a uniform cross-section of 28.6 m², from the inside bottom of the vessel: top of active fuel 9.3 m, normal level 14.3 m, main steam nozzle 16.5 m. Real vessels measure level in the downcomer, against a reference leg. The model stops if the level falls to the top of the fuel or reaches the steam nozzle.
  • The main steam valve stands for the main steam isolation valves together with the turbine control and bypass valves: 2000 kg/s fully open at 7.0 MPa, in proportion to pressure (choked), into a condenser at 0.01 MPa. It strokes at most 20 % per second. Turbine, condenser and reheat are not modelled.
  • Safety/relief valves: eleven, 113 kg/s each at 7.8 MPa, in proportion to pressure, into a suppression pool at 0.1 MPa that does not heat up. Rough sizing figures, not a valve model.
  • Feedwater pumps can deliver up to 2500 kg/s at any vessel pressure; pump head, feedwater heaters and the cleanup (reject) flow are not modelled, so there is no way to drain water during heat-up other than the valves.
  • No vessel or internals heat capacity: the steel would slow heat-up and pressure changes. Generic numbers for a large BWR (about 3300 MW thermal); not any specific plant. All pressures are absolute.
